Although professional football has adopted video assistant referee technology to support offside decisions, this infrastructure remains unavailable outside the elite tier of the sport. This dissertation investigates the feasibility of automated offside detection from broadcast video alone, through the design and evaluation of a modular computer vision pipeline.

The pipeline operates on broadcast clips drawn from the SoccerNet dataset. For each event, the keyframe corresponding to the moment of the pass is manually selected. Players are detected using a YOLO-based object detector, and team affiliation is assigned by clustering kit colours in Lab colour space using K-Means. A homography between the broadcast image plane and a real-world pitch coordinate system is estimated using RANSAC over manually picked pitch landmarks, allowing every player position to be projected onto a metric pitch. The offside condition is then evaluated geometrically by comparing the attacking player against the second-to-last defender along the direction of attack.

Approximately fifty offside events were processed end-to-end and compared against the annotations provided by SoccerNet. The majority of verdicts produced by the system agreed with the dataset annotations, demonstrating that broadcast-only offside detection is feasible on representative footage. The principal failure modes were occasional team misidentification, and small angular deviations in the offside line attributable to imprecision in the estimated homography.

These results suggest that broadcast-only offside detection is achievable with acceptable accuracy at low computational cost. Improving the robustness of team identification and tightening pitch calibration are identified as the next priorities before the system could be deployed without human verification.
